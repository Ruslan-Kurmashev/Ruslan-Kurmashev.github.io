Early Autism Diagnostic Cascade in Kazakhstan

A pilot framework for studying where delays, dropout and referral bottlenecks emerge between first parental concern, screening, specialist assessment and access to support.

ResearchHub reportPeer-reviewed reviewConference paper

Research question

What system-level, family-level, clinical and regional factors determine whether a child with early signs of autism in Kazakhstan reaches timely clinical diagnostic assessment?

The project treats diagnostic delay as a property of a complex health system rather than as the consequence of one missing test. The pathway includes parental concern, primary care, developmental screening, referral, specialist capacity, waiting lists, regional access, stigma, family navigation and intersectoral coordination.

Study design

The current design is a six-month mixed-methods complexity-science pilot. It is observational and does not test a new diagnostic instrument, diagnose children directly or deliver a clinical intervention.

  • Target parent survey: 80–120 parents or legal caregivers of children aged 0–12 years with confirmed or suspected autism who have experience of the Kazakhstani diagnostic pathway.
  • Parent interview subsample: 20–25 families selected to capture variation in region, language, urban/rural residence and pathway experience.
  • Specialist interviews: 10–15 professionals across primary care, child psychiatry, neurology, psychology, speech-language services, educational assessment and autism/NGO services.

Core measures

The study is designed to reconstruct the cascade from first concern to assessment using age and timing at key events, screening and follow-up history, referral source, number of professionals seen, waiting times, region, urban/rural location and reported barriers such as distance, cost, dismissal, stigma or conflicting advice.

Methods

  • Diagnostic cascade analysis and pathway reconstruction.
  • Directed referral-network analysis.
  • Qualitative interviews with families and frontline professionals.
  • Causal-loop diagramming to identify reinforcing and balancing feedback mechanisms.
  • Simple system-dynamics simulation to test practical policy scenarios.

Policy scenarios

Planned scenarios include increasing screening without increasing diagnostic capacity, improving primary-care referral, prioritising younger children, tele-assessment for rural families, a care-navigator model and stronger feedback between screening and specialist services.

Open-science and ethics framework

The design uses informed consent, de-identification, secure data handling and the option to skip questions or withdraw. It does not require child videos, genetic samples, stool samples or identifiable medical records. Planned open outputs include a preregistered protocol, aggregate and synthetic reproducibility data, survey and interview instruments, analysis code, cascade diagrams, a causal-loop model and a policy brief.

Outputs and status

An open ResearchHub report established the project framework. The broader conceptual evidence base is also represented by a peer-reviewed review in Pediatric Investigation and a separate conference-proceedings paper. The empirical mixed-methods pilot remains an active project rather than a completed national estimate.
